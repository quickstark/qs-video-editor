# Wedding editorial contract

Treat a wedding as documentary memory, not a talking-head optimization problem. Protect identity, chronology, emotional pacing, and source authenticity.

## Complete the brief before editing

Record concrete answers for:

- The couple's full display names and pronunciations.
- Wedding date, venue, city, ceremony type, and title-card text.
- Required deliverables: highlight, ceremony, speeches, dances, vertical teaser, or other.
- Target duration, aspect ratio, frame rate, resolution, and delivery codec for each deliverable.
- Priority people and how to identify them across cameras.
- Mandatory moments and acceptable omissions.
- Sensitive moments, guests, minors, or locations that must not appear.
- Desired emotional arc and balance of candid, cinematic, documentary, and energetic material.
- Music selections, licenses, lyrical restrictions, and edit points.
- Whether vows and speeches may be shortened, reordered, or used as voice-over.
- Whether any external generation, transcription, or hosted rendering is authorized.
- Caption language and accessibility expectations.

Do not infer an unanswered identity or consent decision from footage.

## Build story from authentic material

Default highlight structure:

1. Establish place, anticipation, and people.
2. Introduce the couple with authentic voices or actions.
3. Build toward ceremony and vows without spoiling every payoff.
4. Let vows, reactions, and natural sound carry emotional peaks.
5. Release into celebration, speeches, dances, and energy.
6. End on a resolved visual or spoken thought, not a generic montage.

Use this as a starting point, not a mandatory chronology. The brief wins.

## Preserve wedding-specific pacing

- Keep meaningful pauses, breath, room tone, laughter, applause, and reactions.
- Do not remove silence using a global threshold.
- Do not prefer the last take automatically; wedding events are usually unrepeated.
- Do not force the opening to be a marketing hook.
- Do not cut a vow or speech so tightly that cadence, dignity, or meaning changes.
- Keep visual continuity when audio bridges across time.
- Flag, rather than silently repair, contradictions in speaker identity or event order.

## Handle multicamera and audio

- Prefer reliable timecode when cameras and recorders agree.
- Otherwise align waveforms, then verify at the beginning, middle, and end of each long event.
- Preserve the clean recorder as the dialogue source while using camera scratch audio for sync and emergency coverage.
- Record drift corrections explicitly.
- Keep ceremony, speeches, first dance, and open dancing as separate sync groups.
- Maintain room tone and crowd ambience under angle changes.
- Label speakers and priority people in transcripts and markers.

## Keep graphics restrained

Good wedding uses include:

- opening names, date, and venue;
- restrained chapter or location cards;
- lower thirds when the brief requests them;
- an end card or credits;
- maps, family history, or translated text when genuinely informative.

Avoid promotional explainer graphics, constant kinetic text, or overlays that compete with faces and vows.

## Protect documentary truth

- Never synthesize the couple, guests, vows, voices, venue events, or memories.
- Never use generated footage in a way a viewer could mistake for an event that happened.
- Mark generated decorative assets in provenance and keep them visually non-documentary.
- Do not upload footage, faces, voices, guest lists, or private documents without explicit authorization for that provider and purpose.
- Preserve licenses and provider receipts beside each external asset.

## Review identity and privacy

Run a separate story and identity pass that checks:

- the correct person appears with each name and spoken attribution;
- no excluded person or sensitive moment appears;
- required family members and events receive the intended representation;
- reactions are not misleadingly reassigned across moments;
- generated media cannot be confused with documentary footage;
- titles, dates, venue, spelling, and pronunciations match the brief.
